03

Featured Project — PLSS for the MIT BioSuit™

Redesigning a spacesuit’s life-support backpack for a shorter EVA, from subsystem audit to a 3D-printed enclosure.

Background & Objective

A spacesuit is essentially a personal spacecraft: during use it has to maintain pressure, supply oxygen, scrub CO₂, manage heat, and provide power and communications. That hardware and software lives in the Portable Life Support System (PLSS) — in NASA’s current EMU it weighs around 100.5 kg and takes up about 0.11 m³, which limits the astronaut’s mobility and ease of movement.

The objective: design and fabricate a new PLSS for MIT’s BioSuit™, targeting a 50% mass reduction against the current EMU.

Comparison of Existing Suits

What three flight suits actually carry

Before deciding what to remove, the project surveyed what three flight suits carry: the Orlan (Russia’s rear-entry ISS suit), the Apollo-era EMU (NASA’s longstanding main suit), and the AxEMU (Axiom’s next-generation lunar suit).

Key point

All three suits are sized for 7–9 hour microgravity EVAs with full redundancy. Sizing instead for a shorter EVA is where a large part of the mass savings can come from.

Design Decisions

Components modified or removed

Each component in the EMU backpack was checked against a shorter EVA duration instead of the standard 8 hours. Three changes accounted for most of the mass saved — nothing was removed that eliminated a core function.

ComponentMass changeReason
Disposable In-Suit Drink Bag −1 kg A single-use sealed bladder supplying up to 0.946 L of drinking water — about 30% of daily intake. Not needed on a 2-hour EVA, so it moves to the long-duration module instead.
Contaminant Control CartridgeCO₂ scrubber, cut in half −14.97 kg The current METOX cartridge is sized for an 8-hour EVA and takes up 7,400 cm³. A lighter scrubber sized to the shorter EVA removes the heaviest single item in the backpack.
Secondary Oxygen Packreplaced by twin-tank manifold −10.5 kg Borrowing the scuba twin-tank idea, two primary tanks share a high-pressure bridge. If one side fails, isolation valves keep gas in the other tank, so the backup function is kept without a separate pack.

Because the tanks became separate modules, the backpack could be configured per mission: a shorter EVA takes fewer or smaller modules, a longer EVA adds capacity, and maintenance means swapping a tank module rather than taking the core apart.

Compared to the EMU

100.5 kg down to 75 kg, and 0.11 m³ down to 0.09 m³. The backpack is also 17 cm shorter, which matters most for bending over.

CAD & Fabrication

Modelling and printing the enclosure

Modelling — the shell was modelled, then split into printable sections. The curved top follows the shoulder line of the suit, and the lower body is the part that swaps between the short- and long-duration modules.

Printing — parts were split so each could be printed without support on its largest flat face, and so the assembly could be handled by one person.

Skills used — CAD modelling and assemblies, splitting parts for additive manufacturing, print preparation, and fit-checking between printed sections.

Outcome

25.4% lighter, 18% smaller

The BioSuit™ PLSS came out at 75 kg against 100.5 kg for the EMU backpack — a reduction of about 25.4% by mass, with volume down from 0.11 m³ to 0.09 m³. This is short of the 50% target set at the start, but still a meaningful reduction in both mass and size, and it directly supports the goal of improving mobility.

Mars — 2-hour traverse

Two astronauts walk 1.8 km from the habitat to collect rock cores and set up a weather station, with a 30-minute reserve. The smaller lower module covers this case, and the system adds cooling when one astronaut hits loose regolith on the way back.

Moon — 3.5-hour engineering EVA

Hardware testing near the lander. The small lower module is swapped for the larger one, which holds about 4 hours of oxygen and adds drinking water — the case the modular design was built for.
